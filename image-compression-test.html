JPG vs WebP: we measured file size and quality at every setting

Published October 19, 2026 · 4 min read · Download the data (CSV)

Key findings: at the same quality setting, WebP files were 24% smaller than JPG on average at 80% quality, with equal or slightly better measured quality. Matched for visual quality (SSIM), the median saving was 12–21% — and for a few images JPG was smaller. Raising JPG quality from 80% to 95% doubled the file size for a small quality gain.

How we tested

  • Images: nine photographs from the public-domain and openly licensed scikit-image sample images (portraits, animals, objects, astronomy and microscopy), 451 to 1411 pixels wide.
  • Encoders: the browser's built-in JPG and WebP encoders in Chromium 141 — the same encoders CompressZone uses in Chrome and Edge.
  • Settings: every quality level from 30% to 95% in steps of 5 for both formats (252 photo encodes).
  • Quality metric: SSIM (structural similarity) against the lossless original, where 1.0 means identical. Values above about 0.95 are generally hard to tell apart from the original on screen.
  • File size: reported in bits per pixel (bpp) so images of different sizes can be averaged.

What this means

  1. WebP is usually the better choice for the web. At the same setting it produced smaller files in every one of our photos, with equal or slightly higher measured quality.
  2. The real-world saving is smaller than "same setting" comparisons suggest. Because a quality of 80 doesn't mean the same thing in JPG and WebP, we also compared files of equal SSIM. The median saving was 12–21% depending on the quality level, and a few images were smaller as JPG.
  3. 80% is a sensible default. Going from 80% to 95% roughly doubled the size of both JPG and WebP files, while average SSIM rose only from about 0.94 to 0.97.
  4. Never use PNG for photos on the web. Even high-quality JPG was around a tenth of the size.

Limitations

This is a small, reproducible test, not a definitive study. The images are smaller than modern phone photos (usually 12 megapixels or more), where compression savings tend to be larger. SSIM is one of several quality metrics and doesn't capture every visual difference. AVIF isn't included because Chrome cannot encode AVIF from a web page. Results from other encoders, such as libwebp or mozjpeg on the command line, will differ.

Frequently asked questions

Is WebP always smaller than JPG?

No. In our test WebP was smaller at the same quality setting for every photo, but when we matched the visual quality (SSIM), a few images came out smaller as JPG. On average, WebP still saved 12–21%.

What quality setting should I use?

80% gives a good balance for photos on screens. Higher settings grow the file quickly: 95% was about twice the size of 80% in our test.
