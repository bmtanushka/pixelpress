    How JPEG compression works

    JPEG (or JPG) is a lossy image format designed for photographs. When you compress a JPEG, the image is re-encoded at a lower quality setting, which discards fine detail the human eye barely notices. At a quality of 80%, a typical smartphone photo shrinks by 50–80% with no visible difference on screen.

    CompressZone also removes hidden metadata such as camera details and GPS location, which can add tens of kilobytes to each photo. If a JPG is already well compressed and would get bigger, the original file is kept.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the best quality setting for JPEG compression?

    For websites, email and social media, 75–85% quality gives the best balance. 80% is the default. Use 90% or higher for printing, and 60–70% when the smallest possible file matters more than detail.

    Is JPG the same as JPEG?

    Yes. JPG and JPEG are the same format; the shorter .jpg extension comes from older Windows systems that only allowed three-letter extensions.

    How can I reduce a JPEG to under 100 KB or 200 KB?

    Lower the quality to around 60–70% and, if needed, set a maximum dimension such as 1280 or 1920 pixels. Reducing the pixel size has the biggest effect on file size.

    Will compressing a JPEG again make it worse?

    Each lossy re-save can lose a little detail. For the best results, always compress from the original photo rather than from an already-compressed copy.

    Should I convert JPG to WebP instead?

    If the image is for a website, yes: WebP is typically 25–34% smaller than JPG at the same quality and is supported by all modern browsers. Use JPG when you need maximum compatibility, such as email attachments or older software.
